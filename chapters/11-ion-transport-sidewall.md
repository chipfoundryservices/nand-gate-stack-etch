# Chapter 11: Ion Transport & Sidewall Passivation in Deep Holes

## Executive Summary

Chapter 10 derived why etch rate falls with aspect ratio, treating the feature as an idealized, perfectly cylindrical, electrically neutral hole. Real channel holes deviate from this idealization in ways that produce the signature profile defects of extreme-AR etch: bowing (local widening partway down the hole), twisting (lateral drift of the hole's central axis with depth), necking (local constriction), and taper (progressive narrowing with depth). This chapter develops the mechanistic origin of each defect, with particular attention to differential charging — an effect specific to extreme aspect ratio that complicates the pure geometric ion-trajectory picture from Chapter 10, Part 3 by introducing self-reinforcing electric fields generated by the etch process itself. Understanding these mechanisms is a precondition for the targeted mitigation strategies this chapter also develops, each tracing back to a specific root cause rather than being offered as an empirical, unexplained recipe adjustment.

---

## Part 1: Bowing

### 1.1 Mechanistic Origin

Bowing — a local maximum in hole diameter at some intermediate depth, narrower both above and below — arises primarily from a combination of two effects working together: (1) ion-neutral collisions within the upper portion of the feature scatter a fraction of ions at small angles, striking the sidewall at a depth-dependent location with enough residual energy to sputter/etch locally, widening the hole preferentially at the depth where scattered-ion flux is highest; and (2) depletion of sidewall-protecting polymer (Chapter 4, Section 2.3) at this same depth range, if neutral polymer-forming species are being consumed by reaction with the sidewall at a rate that outpaces their delivery to greater depths (Chapter 10, Section 2.2's $\gamma$-dependent attenuation), leaving the sidewall more vulnerable to the scattered-ion attack described in (1) specifically in this intermediate-depth region.

### 1.2 Why Bowing Peaks at an Intermediate Depth, Not at the Top or Bottom

Near the top of the feature, ion density and collision-scattering flux are present but sidewall polymer protection (freshly supplied with abundant neutral flux, Chapter 10, Section 2.1's attenuation not yet severe at shallow depth) is typically still robust, limiting bowing extent. Near the bottom, while sidewall protection may be comparatively thin (per the depth-dependent polymer balance shift, Chapter 10, Section 3.2), the absolute ion flux reaching that depth (both direct and scattered) has itself fallen substantially (Chapter 10, Part 3), reducing the absolute rate of any sidewall sputtering that could occur there. The result is a bowing maximum at an intermediate depth where scattered-ion flux is still appreciable but sidewall protection has already begun to thin — a direct, quantitative consequence of the differently-shaped attenuation curves for direct/scattered ion flux and polymer protection established in Chapter 10.

### 1.3 Bowing as a Yield Risk

Bowing is not merely a cosmetic profile deviation. Chapter 1, Section 7.1 and Section 6.2 established that excessive bowing can cause adjacent channel holes (spaced at the pitch dictated by lithography and bit-density targets) to merge or electrically short at the bowed depth, a catastrophic, non-recoverable failure for both affected strings. Bow magnitude is therefore specified and monitored as a tight critical dimension budget item (commonly specified as maximum allowable bow CD minus nominal CD, in nanometers, at the depth of maximum bow) distinct from, though related to, the top and bottom CD specifications discussed in Chapter 1, Section 4.3.

## Part 2: Twisting

### 2.1 Mechanistic Origin

Twisting — progressive lateral drift of a channel hole's center axis with depth, such that the bottom of the hole is measurably offset from directly beneath the top opening — arises primarily from asymmetric ion trajectory distributions, which can originate from several sources: non-uniform plasma density or sheath thickness across the local feature neighborhood (a highly local analog of the across-wafer uniformity concerns in Chapter 6, Part 2, but operating at the scale of individual features or small feature clusters), charging-induced lateral field asymmetry (Part 3 below), or mask/hard-mask sidewall angle asymmetry at the feature's top opening, which can impart a systematic directional bias to the ion trajectories entering the feature in the first place.

### 2.2 Why Twisting Compounds With Depth

Because twisting reflects an accumulated lateral drift of the etch front's effective center position, any systematic directional bias in incoming ion trajectories (Section 2.1) produces an effect that compounds with depth rather than remaining fixed — a small per-unit-depth lateral drift rate, sustained over several microns of total etch depth, can accumulate into a geometrically significant total lateral offset by the time the etch reaches the bottom of a tall stack. This compounding behavior is directly analogous to, and shares mathematical structure with, random-walk or systematic-drift accumulation problems in other fields, and is a primary reason twisting becomes proportionally more severe (not merely proportionally present) as stack height and layer count increase.

### 2.3 Twisting as a Yield Risk at the Bottom Interface

Severe twisting risks the bottom of the channel hole landing offset from its intended target location at the bottom-of-stack interface (Chapter 2, Section 5.1), potentially missing the intended source contact alignment or landing closer to an adjacent string's intended position than designed. This is a distinct failure mode from bowing's mid-depth shorting risk, instead threatening the bottom contact and process margin specifically, and is addressed through both etch process control (this chapter) and deliberate design margin at the bottom interface (accounting for a characterized, expected twist magnitude in the bottom contact area design, a cross-disciplinary mitigation that connects etch process characterization directly to device layout design rules).

## Part 3: Differential Charging

### 3.1 Why Charging Occurs Specifically at Extreme Aspect Ratio

In a dielectric (oxide/nitride) feature, the sidewall and bottom surfaces are not electrically connected to a conductive path that could rapidly neutralize any accumulated charge. Ions arriving at the bottom of the feature deposit net positive charge there (since ions, not electrons, are the species being deliberately directed downward via the sheath acceleration mechanism, Chapter 7, Section 1.3), while electrons — with far higher thermal velocity but comparatively isotropic (non-directional) angular distribution, and already significantly attenuated at depth by the same general trajectory-filtering logic discussed for ions in Chapter 10, Part 3, but to an even greater degree due to electrons' near-isotropic rather than preferentially-vertical approach angle — struggle to reach the bottom of an extreme-AR feature in matching quantity. This creates a net positive charge imbalance that accumulates specifically at and near the bottom of deep, narrow dielectric features, an effect with negligible consequence at low aspect ratio (where both species reach the bottom in comparable, charge-balanced quantity) but increasingly significant as aspect ratio climbs into the regime Chapter 10 addresses.

### 3.2 How Accumulated Charge Deflects Subsequent Ion Trajectories

The accumulated positive charge at the feature bottom creates a local electric field that repels subsequently arriving positive ions, deflecting their trajectories away from the charge accumulation site rather than allowing them to continue along their original, nominally vertical path. Because this deflection depends on the spatial distribution of accumulated charge, which is itself shaped by the geometry of prior ion impacts, the effect is self-reinforcing: an initially small, random asymmetry in charge accumulation (e.g., from a minor local sidewall irregularity or a slightly asymmetric incoming ion distribution, Section 2.1) can be amplified over the course of continued etching, as the resulting field asymmetry preferentially deflects further ions in a way that reinforces, rather than corrects, the original asymmetry.

### 3.3 Charging as a Primary Twisting and Bowing Amplification Mechanism

This self-reinforcing charging mechanism is now understood as a major contributor to both twisting (Part 2, via sustained lateral deflection) and, in some regimes, bowing (Part 1, via charge-driven redirection of ions toward or away from specific sidewall depths) at the most extreme aspect ratios channel hole etch now operates at — effects that the purely geometric trajectory-filtering model developed in Chapter 10, Part 3 does not, by itself, capture, since that model assumes a fixed, pre-determined angular ion distribution unaffected by feedback from the etch process's own accumulated charge state. This is why Chapter 10's quantitative ARDE model, while an essential and accurate description of average etch rate falloff with depth, must be understood as a necessary but not complete description of extreme-AR etch behavior — profile defect severity, specifically, requires this chapter's charging-augmented picture to fully explain.

### 3.4 Mitigation Strategies for Charging

Several of this book's previously introduced tools gain additional importance specifically as charging mitigation strategies once this chapter's mechanism is understood:

**Pulsed source/bias operation (Chapter 9, Parts 2-4).** Afterglow intervals, during which the continuously-sustained sheath driving directional ion acceleration is interrupted, allow some opportunity for electron flux (less directionally constrained than ion flux, Section 3.1) to partially neutralize accumulated bottom-of-feature charge before the next active/high-bias interval resumes ion-driven charge deposition — directly addressing the charge-accumulation mechanism at its root rather than only its downstream trajectory-deflection consequence.

**Reduced ion energy where the process window permits (Chapter 7, Section 2.1).** Lower ion energy generally reduces the degree to which deflected ion trajectories, once charging-induced deflection begins, translate into significant lateral or depth-wise material removal asymmetry, though this must be balanced against Chapter 7's competing requirement for sufficient ion energy to sustain adequate etch rate (Chapter 10) — charging mitigation via reduced ion energy is therefore a tradeoff lever, not a free improvement.

**Chemistry and sidewall conductivity engineering.** Some process development approaches specifically target sidewall or deposited polymer film properties (Chapter 3, Chapter 4, Section 2.3) that provide some degree of charge leakage/dissipation path along the sidewall, reducing peak accumulated charge density without requiring a fully conductive sidewall (which would be fundamentally incompatible with the dielectric etch target itself).

## Part 4: Necking

### 4.1 Mechanistic Origin

Necking — a local constriction (narrowing) rather than widening of the feature at some depth, the geometric inverse of bowing — arises primarily from excessive, locally concentrated sidewall polymer deposition at a specific depth, typically near a transition point in the recipe's chemistry or power ramping schedule (Chapter 3, Section 4.2; Chapter 7, Section 3.2) where the delivered polymer-forming neutral flux temporarily exceeds the ion-assisted clearing capacity available at that depth, producing a locally thicker protective layer than the surrounding depths above and below experience.

### 4.2 Necking's Distinct Risk Profile

Where bowing threatens adjacent-hole shorting (Section 1.3) and twisting threatens bottom-contact misalignment (Section 2.3), necking primarily threatens the mechanical and electrical continuity of the channel polysilicon and ONO stack that must subsequently be deposited through the constricted region (Chapter 2, Section 4.2-4.3) — a severe necking defect can leave insufficient opening for void-free ONO/polysilicon fill, producing a local resistance bottleneck or outright electrical discontinuity in the finished string even if the etch itself technically clears to the bottom of the stack.

### 4.3 Why Necking Is Often a Recipe-Transition Artifact

Because necking frequently correlates with specific recipe step transitions (Section 4.1) rather than arising continuously throughout the etch the way bowing and twisting tend to, necking is often diagnosed and corrected through careful recipe transition tuning — adjusting the timing, rate, and overlap of gas chemistry and power ramps at step boundaries (Chapter 3, Section 4.2; Chapter 9) specifically to avoid the transient polymer-flux mismatch that Section 4.1 identifies as the root mechanism, rather than requiring a change to the bulk, steady-state portion of the recipe at all.

## Part 5: Taper

### 5.1 Mechanistic Origin and Distinction From Bowing

Taper — a monotonic, progressive narrowing (or, less commonly, widening) of the feature with depth, without the local maximum/minimum character of bowing or necking — is the most direct, continuous expression of Chapter 10's attenuation physics: as both ion and neutral flux progressively decline with depth (Chapter 10, Parts 2-3), and as the balance between lateral (sidewall) and vertical (bottom) etch rate shifts gradually rather than abruptly, the feature's diameter at depth $z$ reflects the cumulative effect of this gradually shifting balance integrated from the top of the feature down to $z$, typically producing a smooth, monotonic CD trend rather than a localized bow or neck.

### 5.2 Taper as the Baseline the Other Defects Are Superimposed Upon

In practice, measured channel hole profiles typically show taper as the dominant, large-scale trend, with bowing, necking, and twisting (Parts 1, 2, 4) superimposed as more localized deviations from this smooth taper baseline. Separating a measured profile into its taper component (addressed primarily through Chapter 10's depth-compensation strategies) and its superimposed localized defect components (addressed through this chapter's charging, polymer-balance, and recipe-transition mitigation strategies) is standard practice in profile metrology analysis (Chapter 15), since the two categories of deviation generally respond to different corrective levers and should not be conflated when diagnosing a specific process excursion.

## Part 6: A Worked Charging Field Estimate

### 6.1 Order-of-Magnitude Deflection Estimate

To make Section 3.2's charging-deflection mechanism quantitative, consider a simplified estimate of the lateral electric field produced by a modest accumulated charge imbalance at the bottom of a feature. Treating the accumulated charge as a localized surface charge density $\sigma_q$ over the feature's bottom area, the resulting field at a point just above the bottom (within the feature, at the length scale relevant to deflecting subsequently arriving ions) scales approximately as:

$$E \approx \frac{\sigma_q}{2\epsilon_0}$$

**Illustrative calculation:** suppose charge imbalance accumulates at a rate corresponding to a net ion current density imbalance of order $10^{-4}\ \text{A/cm}^2$ (a small fraction of typical total ion current density delivered to the wafer, consistent with only a modest, localized asymmetry in an otherwise largely charge-balanced system) sustained over a characteristic charging timescale of order 1 ms before some discharge/neutralization process (Section 3.4) limits further accumulation:

$$\sigma_q \approx J \times t \approx 10^{-4}\ \text{A/cm}^2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s} = 10^{-7}\ \text{C/cm}^2 = 10^{-3}\ \text{C/m}^2$$

$$E \approx \frac{10^{-3}}{2 \times 8.85\times10^{-12}} \approx 5.6\times10^{7}\ \text{V/m}$$

A field of this order, acting over even a short deflection path length comparable to the feature's own sub-150nm diameter, is sufficient to impart significant lateral deflection to an ion with the energies typical of channel hole etch bias conditions (hundreds of eV, Chapter 7, Section 5.3) — this order-of-magnitude estimate is consistent with charging being recognized industry-wide as a first-order, not merely secondary, contributor to twisting and bowing severity at extreme aspect ratio, and explains why even a comparatively small fractional charge imbalance (far smaller than the total ion flux reaching the feature bottom) can produce a measurable, yield-relevant profile deflection.

### 6.2 Why This Estimate Explains the Observed Aspect-Ratio Threshold for Charging Significance

This chapter's charging mechanism requires both (a) a meaningful ion-electron flux imbalance reaching the bottom of the feature (negligible at low aspect ratio, per Section 3.1, but increasingly significant at the aspect ratios Chapter 10 addresses) and (b) insufficient charge dissipation path to neutralize the resulting accumulation before it affects subsequent ion trajectories. Because both conditions sharpen simultaneously as aspect ratio increases past roughly the 30:1-50:1 range (consistent with Chapter 10, Section 6.1's generational aspect ratio progression), charging-driven profile defects are widely reported in the industry literature as becoming a first-order concern specifically from the 64-96 layer generation range onward — broadly consistent with this chapter's qualitative mechanism and this section's order-of-magnitude field estimate, even though exact onset thresholds are reactor- and process-specific and not reducible to a single universal aspect ratio value.

## Part 7: Profile Defect Diagnostic Summary

### 7.1 Distinguishing Defects by Measured Signature

Production metrology (Chapter 15) must distinguish between these distinct defect types from cross-sectional profile measurements (typically via destructive cross-section SEM/TEM on sampled wafers, or non-destructive scatterometry-based CD metrology at multiple depths) in order to route each observed excursion to the correct corrective lever:

| Defect | Profile Signature | Primary Root Cause (This Chapter) | Primary Corrective Lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bowing | Local CD maximum at intermediate depth | Scattered-ion sidewall attack coinciding with locally thinned polymer protection (Part 1) | Chemistry/pressure tuning to shift polymer balance at the affected depth; pulsing duty cycle adjustment |
| Twisting | Progressive lateral centerline offset with depth, accumulating toward the bottom | Charging-amplified asymmetric ion deflection (Part 2, Part 3) | Pulsed/synchronized bias operation (Chapter 9) to allow charge dissipation; mask/top-opening symmetry improvement |
| Necking | Local CD minimum at a specific depth, often near a recipe step transition | Transient polymer-flux/clearing-rate mismatch at step boundaries (Part 4) | Recipe transition timing and ramp-rate tuning (Chapter 3, Section 4.2; Chapter 9) |
| Taper | Smooth, monotonic CD trend with depth, no local extremum | Direct expression of Chapter 10's gradual ion/neutral flux attenuation | Depth-ramped power compensation (Chapter 7, Section 3.2; Chapter 10, Section 5.1) |

### 7.2 Why Multiple Defects Typically Co-Occur

In practice, a single measured channel hole profile typically shows some combination of all four signatures superimposed (Section 5.2), since the underlying mechanisms (flux attenuation, charging, polymer balance, recipe transitions) are all simultaneously active throughout any real etch process. Process debug and optimization work therefore generally proceeds by first correcting the largest-magnitude, most yield-impactful component (frequently taper or bowing, given their direct connection to Chapter 1's acceptance criteria) before fine-tuning the secondary, smaller-magnitude components, rather than attempting to address all four defect categories through a single, simultaneous recipe change.

## Summary and Forward Look

Real channel hole profiles deviate from Chapter 10's idealized cylindrical transport picture through bowing (scattered-ion/polymer-depletion-driven local widening), twisting (compounding lateral drift, strongly amplified by differential charging), necking (localized polymer-flux mismatch, often at recipe transitions), and taper (the smooth, monotonic baseline reflecting Chapter 10's gradual attenuation physics directly). Differential charging — a mechanism specific to the extreme aspect ratios this book addresses, arising from the fundamental difference in how directionally-constrained ions versus comparatively isotropic electrons populate the bottom of a deep dielectric feature — is a primary amplifier of both twisting and bowing, and is mitigated primarily through pulsed operation strategies that interrupt the continuous charge-accumulation process.

With both the average etch-rate behavior (Chapter 10) and the profile-defect mechanisms (this chapter) established, the next chapter turns to a related but distinct process control question this book has referenced but not yet treated in full: how selectivity between oxide, nitride, and polysilicon is engineered and maintained across the many alternating film transitions a single channel hole etch traverses, and how the averaged-selectivity framework introduced conceptually in Chapter 4, Section 4.2 is developed into a complete, quantitative process control methodology.
